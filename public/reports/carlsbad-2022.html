Carlsbad 2022: Executive Summary

Housing Advocacy Intelligence Report · NC YIMBY

Data caveat: The council record for 2022 is severely incomplete. Multiple bodies produced only one or two meeting records for the entire year. This summary reflects available data; significant council activity almost certainly occurred outside this window. No etrakit development pipeline data was provided for this period.

Carlsbad's 2022 was a year of quiet, structurally significant code reform at the Planning Commission level, competent affordable housing administration, and a City Council largely invisible in the housing record. The Planning Commission approved every housing project that reached a vote and implemented two major zoning cleanups expanding where housing can be built. The council's own record is paper-thin: across all bodies and meeting packets, no contested housing project vote, no tenant protection measure, and no RHNA compliance action surfaces at the council level. The most consequential housing policy of the year happened in the code, not in a headline vote.

Zoning and Density

The Planning Commission drove two structural changes that expanded Carlsbad's housing capacity:

Housing Element Code Amendments (March): The commission amended the zoning code to require only objective design standards for affordable housing projects (SB 35/SB 330 compliance), allowed both vertical and horizontal mixed-use residential in C-1, C-2, and C-L commercial zones at a minimum of 15 du/acre, and enabled building permit self-certification. Opening commercial zones to residential at 15+ du/acre is a genuine capacity expansion; it creates housing sites that did not previously exist in the zoning code.

Zoning Ordinance Cleanup (July, 7-0): Removed the Growth Management Program control point from the nonconforming use definition, so projects above the control point but within General Plan density range are no longer treated as nonconforming. Added employee housing (6 or fewer persons) as a permitted use in all residential zones per Health & Safety Code 17021.5. Updated family day care to by-right per SB 234.

The growth management control point change deserves emphasis. Carlsbad's voter-approved Growth Management Program (Proposition E) assigns dwelling unit caps by zone. Projects exceeding the control point were previously classified as nonconforming uses, a designation that limits expansion, financing, and reconstruction rights. Removing that classification, while keeping the tracking, is a meaningful reduction in regulatory friction. However, staff language reveals the city's posture: units continue to be tracked against the control point "in the event the city has the authority to use the Growth Management dwelling unit caps in the future" (City Planner Neu). The city is complying with SB 330's prohibition on development moratoria while preserving the infrastructure to reimpose caps if state law changes.

The council discussed amendments to the Density Bonus Ordinance and ADU regulations requiring Coastal Commission certification with suggested modifications to the Local Coastal Program. No final votes appear in the record; these were at the introduction stage.

Inclusionary in-lieu fee restructured (approved March 22, effective April 1): shifted from flat per-unit to tiered. Single-family: $8,529/unit. Projects of 2-6 units: $15/sq ft. Affordable Housing Impact Fee held at $2,925/unit. These rates remain low compared to peer coastal San Diego jurisdictions.

Development Pipeline

No etrakit discretionary filing or building permit data was provided for 2022, limiting pipeline analysis. From budget documents: 696 residential units were entitled during the year, with 116 dedicated affordable to lower-income families, and 419 affordable units in various stages from construction to permitting.

The Traffic & Mobility Commission's Growth Management Program administration is the key pipeline constraint. When Aviara Parkway was found operating at LOS F, the commission voted 6-0 to exempt it from the vehicular LOS standard rather than trigger development restrictions. This follows a pattern: 32-33 street facilities have been exempted over the years as they fail. Vice-Chair Linke noted the tension directly: "whenever a facility fails, we are just exempting it." HCD's April 2020 opinion found such moratoria impermissible under SB 330, making the exemptions legally compelled rather than discretionary pro-housing action.

The Water District's drought contingency plan adopted a six-level framework that suspends new water connections, meter installations, and will-serve letters at Drought Level 3 and above. This is a legitimate resource constraint, not an obstruction tactic, but it represents a real bottleneck for entitled projects without building permits during water emergencies.

Tenant Protections

No tenant protection measures (rent stabilization, relocation assistance, just-cause eviction) appear in any body's 2022 record. This absence may reflect the thin data rather than policy inaction.

State Mandate Compliance

The council endorsed two Housing Element site selection maps for further environmental review. A Growth Management Plan update was underway via citizen committee. Objective design standards were in development citywide and for the Village & Barrio Master Plan. Permit-ready ADU plans were planned. These are forward-looking compliance measures, but no final Housing Element adoption or RHNA progress report surfaces in the record.

SB 330 was repeatedly acknowledged as superseding local growth caps, both at Planning Commission and Traffic & Mobility Commission. The VMT analysis guidelines adopted SB 743 screening exemptions that are pro-housing in effect: projects within half-mile of Coaster stations, affordable housing, and local-serving retail under 50,000 sq ft are screened out of CEQA transportation analysis. Linke raised legitimate concerns that the screening methodology was so broad that "almost every project gets screened out for the mitigation."

Affordable Housing Finance

Carlsbad's affordable housing administration was the strongest visible element of its 2022 housing record:

  • $5.25M committed from Housing Trust Fund to three projects
  • Windsor Pointe: 50 affordable apartments for homeless veterans and individuals with mental health needs (land acquisition and construction loan completed)
  • Tyler Court Senior Apartments: 75 city-owned units, 100% affordable at 30% and 50% AMI
  • Bressi Ranch: 11 city-owned condos sold to lower-income households
  • Section 8: 550 households receiving monthly rental subsidies, program expanding ($10.4M, federally funded)
  • CDBG: $637K regular allocation plus $1.2M COVID response
  • Affordable Housing Fund: $18.4M ending balance
  • Homelessness services: 700+ individuals served, 40+ placed in shelters, 40+ in permanent housing

La Posada de Guadalupe emergency shelter (120-bed low-barrier facility): council approved 4-1 a clinician services grant with Catholic Charities. Blackburn dissented, opposing the low-barrier model because it "allows residents to be under the influence upon arrival." Low-barrier shelters are the evidence-based approach; opposing the model is opposing what works.

Infrastructure and Budget

Rail trenching study ($3M from General Fund reserves): Norby moved, Blackburn seconded. Failed 2-2-1 (Bhat-Patel and Acosta no, Hall abstained). Rail trenching affects long-term development capacity along the corridor.

Sustainable Mobility Plan: Norby moved to remove from deceleration list. Approved 3-1 (Blackburn no, Hall absent).

Traffic Impact Fee had not been updated in 13+ years. A new multi-modal fee study was initiated. Development impact fees increased 7.6% per ENR Construction Cost Index.

Housing & Homeless Services department budget cut 46.9% ($5.9M to $3.1M) while staffing increased from 15 to 16 FTE; the reduction appears driven by reclassification rather than service cuts.

STR enforcement strengthened with escalating fines up to $5,000 per violation and civil penalties up to $2,500/day ($400,000 cap per parcel). A new "notice of ineligibility for land development" tool blocks all permits on properties with unresolved code violations.

Council Member Patterns

Data is too thin for confident pattern assessment. What is visible:

Blackburn (Mayor Pro Tem): Opposed low-barrier shelter model (sole dissent). Opposed Sustainable Mobility Plan reprioritization. Supported rail trenching. The shelter vote is the clearest signal: opposing evidence-based homelessness intervention.

Norby: Championed both rail trenching and Sustainable Mobility Plan. Most infrastructure-forward member in available records.

Hall (Mayor): Voted no on budget resolutions. Recused on Village/Barrio items (conflict of interest). Abstained on rail trenching. Pattern unclear from limited data.

Bhat-Patel: Opposed rail trenching funding. Supported mobility plan.

Acosta: Opposed rail trenching funding.

Lafferty (Planning Commission): Only commissioner to dissent on a housing project (Garfield Beach, 12 units). Consistently raised density and design concerns but never blocked a project beyond the single no vote.

Linke (Traffic & Mobility Commission Vice-Chair): Most analytically rigorous voice on growth management. Drove MMLOS accountability, flagged VMT screening methodology concerns, identified consultant conflicts of interest. Dissents were about incomplete reporting, not about restricting development.

Bottom line: Carlsbad's 2022 shows a city making structurally significant code changes (commercial zone housing, growth management control point reform) while its council remained largely invisible on housing. The Planning Commission did the heavy lifting: approving every project, implementing Housing Element obligations, and expanding where housing can be built. The affordable housing pipeline ($5.25M in trust fund commitments, 419 affordable units in progress, 550 Section 8 households) reflects competent administration of existing programs. What is absent from the record is as notable as what is present: no tenant protections, no contested council-level housing votes, no visible RHNA progress reporting, and a growth management infrastructure kept carefully intact for possible future reimposition. The city is complying with state law. Whether it is leading on housing, or simply not obstructing, cannot be determined from this record.
