What the subsidiary records reveal is a city processing housing under state legal compulsion rather than local policy initiative: the one significant project approval cited the Housing Accountability Act as the binding authority, and the year's major policy exercise was drafting objective design standards because state law prohibited enforcing the subjective ones.

Zoning and Density: The Growth Management Program's Quiet Death

The most consequential structural finding of 2023 surfaced not through a vote but through institutional acknowledgment. The Traffic & Mobility Commission reviewed the FY 2021-22 Growth Management Plan monitoring report, which documented that HCD's April 2020 opinion rendered Carlsbad's traffic-based building permit moratorium unenforceable under SB 330.

The Growth Management Program historically allowed the city to halt building permits in zones where traffic fell below Level of Service D. HCD rejected this mechanism outright: "HCD does not consider...that general concerns about the health and welfare of the citizenry, including traffic conditions that cause minor delays, present an imminent threat to health and safety." HCD warned that temporarily prohibiting multifamily development was "a serious constraint and contrary to planning and zoning law."

Ten street segments fell below LOS D in 2022 monitoring. All are designated exempt under Mobility Element Policy 3-P.9. The moratorium trigger cannot legally fire on any of them. Carlsbad's most powerful growth-control tool, the mechanism that made traffic congestion a veto over housing permits, is dead. The commission received this as information with no recorded opposition, suggesting institutional acceptance.

Separately, the city began developing a Multimodal Transportation Impact Fee to replace the existing vehicle-only Traffic Impact Fee. No fee amounts were set. A shift from vehicle-LOS to multimodal analysis could reduce impact fees for dense, transit-oriented projects by removing the penalty for not generating car trips. This remains a future item with no 2023 action.

Objective Design Standards. The Village & Barrio Design Review Committee spent its one substantive meeting (May 25) reviewing draft Objective Design Standards for multifamily and mixed-use development in the Village and Barrio Master Plan area. Staff stated explicitly that existing subjective design guidelines "cannot be enforced for eligible projects" under HAA, SB 35, and SB 330. The standards covered architectural styles, frontage types, building massing, and a ministerial approval pathway.

Patrick Connolly pushed to retain discretionary review for Traditional Modern and California Contemporary architectural styles, arguing they lacked sufficient objective criteria for ministerial processing. Alicia Lafferty took the opposite position, warning that rejecting or fragmenting the standards would leave Carlsbad with no baseline for navigating streamlined permitting and density bonus requirements. The committee forwarded recommendations to the Planning Commission; no final vote was recorded.

Connolly's instinct to preserve discretionary review for certain styles cuts directly against the ministerial pathway state law mandates. Maintaining discretionary review where the state requires objective standards creates HAA and SB 35 litigation exposure.

State Mandate Compliance

Carlsbad's 2023 posture toward state housing law was compliance without enthusiasm. Two patterns emerged:

First, the city treated state law as a binding constraint on its own authority. Senior Assistant City Attorney Kemp cited the Housing Accountability Act as requiring approval of the FPC Residential project. Staff told the Design Review Committee that subjective design guidelines were unenforceable under HAA, SB 35, and SB 330. Transportation Director Frank acknowledged density mandates: "It is getting more challenging with the state laws coming out about higher densities." The consistent framing was adaptation to external requirements, not local policy choice.

Second, the GMP moratorium's death under SB 330 was accepted without challenge. The prior litigation (North County Advocates v. City of Carlsbad, settled 2017) had already tested Carlsbad's growth management framework. HCD's opinion closed the remaining door.

Affordable Housing Finance

Infrastructure and Budget

El Camino Real widening (Sunny Creek Rd to Jackspar Dr) received coastal development permit, hillside development permit, and General Plan open space policy waiver approval (Council, 5-0). Avenida Encinas Coastal Rail Trail improvements were approved 7-0, with Coastal Commission coordination noted. $200M Sprinter double tracking along Oceanside Boulevard was discussed (SANDAG estimate) but not acted on. Bike and pedestrian safety infrastructure dominated the Traffic Commission's work, responding to the city's August 2022 local emergency declaration following a 200%+ increase in bike/e-bike collisions since 2019.

A failed motion for a park in Planning Area F Southwest Quadrant (2-3: Acosta and Bhat-Patel yes; Blackburn, Burkholder, and Luna no) was the only recorded contested Council vote. Burkholder argued the motion ignored a transportation project and coordination with state entities.

Council Member Patterns

The absence of City Council meeting records makes systematic assessment impossible. What the cross-referenced records show:

Council votes appeared only in Traffic Commission agenda packets: the park motion (failed 2-3), El Camino Real widening (5-0), and the affordable housing resale (5-0). The park vote is the only contested split, and it is not a housing item.

Planning Commission members are better documented. The unanimous FPC Residential approval with supportive commentary from Stine, Sabellico, Meenes, and Kamenjarin reflects a commission willing to approve density-bonus housing. Lafferty's sole dissent (Edwards Residence, on variance precedent grounds) was narrow and procedural, not anti-housing.

Design Review Committee showed the clearest ideological split: Connolly seeking to preserve discretionary design review authority vs. Lafferty warning that incomplete objective standards would leave the city exposed under state streamlining law. This is the year's clearest proxy for the local-control-vs-compliance tension, though the committee is advisory only.
