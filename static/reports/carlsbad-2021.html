Carlsbad 2021: Executive Summary

Housing Advocacy Intelligence Report · NC YIMBY

Carlsbad's 2021 was defined by one structural question: whether a city 90% built out, operating under a voter-approved growth cap, would dismantle the cap or cling to it. The answer was mixed. The council declared its Growth Management Plan housing caps unenforceable under state law, and the draft Housing Element proposed genuine density increases and new high-density zoning designations. But no housing projects were denied or approved by council vote in the record, no tenant protections were adopted, and the inclusionary in-lieu fee remained at $4,515 per unit, a fraction of what peer cities charge. Carlsbad in 2021 was a city planning to build on paper while deferring every hard decision to future years.

Zoning and Density

Growth Management Plan caps declared unenforceable. Council Resolution 2021-074 (April 6, 2021) found that Carlsbad's residential housing caps under Proposition E (1986), which limit citywide buildout to 54,599 dwelling units across four quadrants, are preempted by SB 330's prohibition on policies that cap housing production. Staff stated plainly that "the city needs to plan for more growth than is currently planned for." The resolution did not repeal the GMP; it acknowledged state law overrides it. Whether the council will seek voter approval to formally eliminate the cap, or simply rely on state preemption until SB 330 sunsets in 2025, remained unresolved.

New high-density zoning designations proposed. The draft 6th Cycle Housing Element created two new residential categories: R-35 (32.5-35 du/ac) and R-40 (37.5-40 du/ac), both with 50-foot height limits, specifically to accommodate lower-income RHNA. Minimum densities were raised across existing zones: R-15 from 8.0 to 11.5 du/ac, R-23 from 15.0 to 19.0 du/ac, R-30 from 23.0 to 26.5 du/ac. These are meaningful increases that close the gap between zoned capacity and actual production requirements.

Major rezoning program committed. The Housing Element identified 6,472 potential units across rezoning categories: six industrial sites (757 units), two commercial sites (471 units), 13 R-4 single-family parcels upzoned to multi-family (625-875 units), and city/agency-owned properties including the Shoppes at Carlsbad (57 acres, ~993 units) and NCTD's Carlsbad Village Train Station (7.8 acres, ~94 units). Greystar, Brookfield, and NCTD submitted letters supporting residential redesignation of their parcels. Deadline: April 2024.

Excess Dwelling Unit Bank recommended for deletion. The system that previously banked and rationed development capacity was flagged for elimination as incompatible with SB 330. This is a structural removal of a growth-limiting mechanism.

Intense public opposition to specific upzoning sites. Site 13/Zone 20 in the Aviara area (six parcels, ~21 acres, proposed rezone from R-4 to R-23/R-30 for up to 327 units) drew organized resistance. Attorney Reza Wrathall argued one parcel is landlocked. DeAnn Weimer of Friends of Aviara cited Coastal Act habitat protections. Larry Cohen of Aviara Premier Association challenged notification procedures. At Ponto, Lance Schulte and People for Ponto argued the Coastal Commission never certified the proposed land use change and that the city never completed a required visitor-serving use inventory. The opposition deployed every available legal argument: habitat, Coastal Act, traffic, fire evacuation, school capacity, property values.

Direction: pro-housing on paper. New density designations, minimum density increases, industrial/commercial rezoning, growth cap acknowledgment. All structural changes point toward expanded capacity. None were adopted by vote in 2021; they remained Housing Element commitments with future implementation dates.

Development Pipeline

No discretionary project filing or building permit trend data was provided for 2021. The Planning Commission approved one project: Garfield Beach Homes (3570-3590 Garfield St), a 12-unit condominium at 21-23 du/ac in the Beach Area Overlay Zone, demolishing 3 existing units for a net gain of 9. Chair Joseph Stine signed the resolution; no dissents recorded. Staff explicitly cited SB 330 to confirm the Growth Management Control Point cannot function as a housing cap. The project's two affordable units were satisfied through credit purchase from the Tavarua credit bank (26 credits remaining). The Housing Element identified 1,821 units across 100 approved-but-unbuilt projects, including Carlsbad Village Lofts (106 units), Marja Acres (294 units), North County Plaza (272 units), Uptown Bressi (125 units), and Carlsbad Station (79 units).

Tenant Protections

None. No rent stabilization, relocation assistance, or just-cause eviction measures appear anywhere in the 2021 record. For a city with fair housing testing showing differential treatment at rental sites and 95-99% of fair housing complaints coming from residents below 80% of poverty level (concentrated in zip codes 92008 and 92009), the absence of tenant protection activity is a gap.

State Mandate Compliance

Carlsbad's posture was cooperative but incomplete. The city is subject to SB 35 streamlined approval after failing to meet 5th Cycle RHNA for lower-income categories. Performance was lopsided: above-moderate production hit 131% of target (3,056 of 2,332), while lower-income reached only 35%. The 6th Cycle RHNA allocation is 3,873 units. The draft Housing Element commits to objective design standards (Program 1.11), by-right supportive housing per AB 2162, and Low-Barrier Navigation Centers per AB 101. ADU production is projected at 75 units/year (502 total over the cycle), a 3x multiplier over the historical 19/year baseline. HCD certified the element in compliance. The Growth Management Plan conflict with SB 330 is explicitly acknowledged, with a program requiring resolution before the 2025 sunset, though the element hedges that voter approval may be needed.

Affordable Housing Finance

Housing Trust Fund: $12.4M-$17.8M (figures vary across reports; the range reflects different reporting dates). Generates $1-2M annually. Inclusionary requirement: 15% for projects of 7+ units, restored January 2020 via Ordinance CS-368 after AB 1505 reversed the Palmer decision. Approximately 2,300 affordable units produced through inclusionary since 1993. Inclusionary in-lieu fee: $4,515/unit, flagged in public comment as dramatically below peer cities (Laguna Beach charges ~$160,000/unit). No action taken on fee reform despite ~$19M accumulated in inclusionary fee accounts. Section 8: ~$6-6.6M/year serving ~550 families monthly. Annual CDBG: ~$500K. Pacific Wind affordable housing received a $33M tax-exempt bond TEFRA hearing (93 units, no city repayment obligation). Per-unit development fees: $42,617 single-family, $16,762 multi-family, including $2,915 affordable housing impact fee.

Key affordable pipeline: Pacific Wind (87 units, 100% affordable, $7.4M city investment, but financing unsuccessful as of June 2020), Windsor Pointe (50 units, homeless veterans, $8.3M), Quarry Creek (64 units, $1.3M), Tyler Court (37 extremely low-income senior units), Harding/Oak (50 units, $165,868/unit subsidy). Santa Fe Ranch lost 64 affordable units when owners paid off bonds in 2016 and declined to extend deed restrictions.

Infrastructure and Budget

The Carlsbad Municipal Water District approved Drought Response Conservation Ordinance No. 44 (5-0). During a Level 3 declaration, no new water connections would be issued except for projects with existing building permits. Not invoked in 2021, but a latent development constraint. Wastewater CIP: $88M over five years, $221M over 14 years. Recycled water program shows debt service coverage ratios of 0.42x-0.73x against a 1.0x covenant, with the implemented 4% rate increase far below the 46-177% calculated as necessary. Water/wastewater rates had not increased for years, leaving them lowest in San Diego County. A $10.7M General Fund transfer to infrastructure replacement was proposed. El Camino Real widening projects (CIP 6072, CIP 6094) expand road capacity supporting development corridors.

Council Member Vote Patterns

No individual council member votes are documented in any contested item in the 2021 record. The structural decisions (Resolution 2021-074 on growth cap preemption, Housing Element commitments) lack recorded vote splits. The only named officials voting on anything are Planning Commission and Traffic and Mobility Commission members. Commissioner Linke consistently pushed for infrastructure accountability: his motion for an incremental Traffic Impact Fee update (the project list hadn't been updated since 2008) failed, and he documented that staff replaced council-adopted VMT analysis maps with less rigorous SANDAG maps without public review, allowing at least one project to avoid CEQA transportation analysis. Linke was elected vice-chair and nominated to the Growth Management Plan Update Advisory Committee. Commissioner Perez was elected chair. Chair Gocan resigned. The thin voting record makes individual assessment impossible for council members.

Bottom line: Carlsbad in 2021 acknowledged its growth cap is unenforceable, proposed genuine density increases and rezoning, and maintained a well-funded affordable housing trust. But the hard votes (actually repealing the growth cap, raising the in-lieu fee from $4,515, adopting tenant protections, completing the April 2024 rezoning) were all deferred. The 5th Cycle record (131% above-moderate, 35% lower-income) reveals a city that builds luxury housing while underproducing affordable units. Whether the Housing Element's commitments translate to production depends entirely on future council action. Planning to build is not building.
